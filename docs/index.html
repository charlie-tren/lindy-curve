The Lindy Curve

Old things are supposed to endure: what has lasted a century is a fair bet to last another. Books make that testable, because Project Gutenberg publishes how often each of its texts was read this month. Here are 67,488 of them, by age.

What it actually says

Nothing much, and that is the finding. The correlation is +0.110 over the whole corpus, -0.104 once the baseline band is excluded, and back across zero above that. A relationship whose sign depends on an arbitrary cutoff is not a relationship.

What this cannot show

The books that died are missing. A catalogue of survivors holds no failures: all 947 works over five hundred years old are here because someone chose to digitise them. That alone is reason not to read a verdict off these charts.

Dates are the author's, not the work's - Gutenberg publishes no publication date, so they come from the author's lifespan capped at birth plus fifty.

The count is a rolling thirty days with an undocumented floor - 61.5% of the corpus sits between 300 and 600 a month, too uniform to be readers. Hence the slider.

Public domain only, so nothing after 1929, and 134 works span 500 to 1300 AD against 27,719 from the 1800s alone. 78% is English, and editions of one work are separate records, which splits its readers.

A second opinion

Gutenberg's count has a floor - 61.5% of the corpus sits between 300 and 600 a month, too uniform to be readers - so the finding needs a measure that does not share the flaw. Wikipedia pageviews have no floor and run monthly back to 2016, which also gives the multi-year average Gutenberg cannot.
