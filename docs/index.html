The Lindy Curve

Homer is still read after 2,500 years; most books are forgotten inside ten. Gutenberg publishes how many people opened each of its texts this month, so the claim that survivors keep surviving can be checked. Here are 67,488 books, by age.

What this cannot show

The books that died are missing. A catalogue of survivors holds no failures. All 947 works over five hundred years old are here because someone chose to digitise them, so the ancient end is pre-selected for endurance in a way the 1900s is not. That alone is reason not to read a verdict off these charts.

Dates are the author's, not the work's. Gutenberg publishes no publication date, so they come from the author's lifespan, capped at birth plus fifty.

The readership count is a rolling thirty days, and it has a floor. 61.5% of the corpus sits between 300 and 600 a month, far too uniform across 51,520 mostly obscure works to be human readership, and Gutenberg does not document what it is. Raise the bar on the second chart above 600 and you are past it.

Public domain only, so nothing after 1929 - which is where most forgetting actually happens. 134 works span 500 to 1300 AD against 27,719 from the 1800s alone, and 78% is English. Editions and translations of one work are separate records, which splits its readers across several entries.

The Wikipedia figures are worse on all of this, not better. Only the works canonical enough to have an article can be matched at all, so that chart concentrates the same survivorship bias rather than escaping it.

Built from one request to Gutenberg's RDF feed plus its catalogue CSV for subjects, computed at build time, Spearman with tie correction because the floor creates tens of thousands of ties. Rebuilt weekly. Source on GitHub.
