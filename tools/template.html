The Lindy Curve

Old things are supposed to endure: what has lasted a century is a fair bet to last another. Books make that testable, because Project Gutenberg publishes how often each of its texts was read this month. Here are %%WORKS%% of them, by age.

Age vs downloads, Spearman +0.110

What the measurement actually says

Nothing much, and that is the finding. Across the whole corpus the correlation is +0.110 - older works read slightly more. Exclude the baseline band and it falls to -0.101 at a thousand downloads a month, then wanders back across zero as the threshold climbs. A relationship whose sign depends on where you draw an arbitrary line is not a relationship. On this evidence, how old a surviving book is tells you almost nothing about how much it is read.

Which is not quite the same as saying the Lindy effect is wrong. It says this dataset cannot test it - and the reason is in the methodology below, because the books that failed the test are not in the data at all.

The middle half of each century

The band is the middle 50% of works from that century - a quarter read less, a quarter read more - and the line is the median. This replaced a ridgeline, which stacked the same distributions on top of each other and made them almost impossible to compare. Here the answer is legible in one look: the bands sit at the same height across two and a half thousand years. Centuries with fewer than five works are left out, and the counts are on the axis so you can see which are thin.

What this can and cannot show

The honest limits, in rough order of how much they matter. Any one of them is enough reason not to read a verdict on the Lindy effect off these charts.

The books that died are not in the data

This is the big one, and it cannot be fixed. The Lindy claim is about survival, but a catalogue of surviving texts contains no failures. Gutenberg holds %%ANCIENT%% works older than five hundred years, and every one of them is there because somebody thought it worth digitising. The tens of thousands of ancient works that were lost, and the mass of forgotten recent ones that were not, are both absent - in opposite directions. Plotting the survivors and calling the result a decay curve would be measuring a selection decision, not the passage of time.

The dates are the author's, not the work's

Gutenberg publishes no composition or first-publication date - its issued field is when the file went online. So every date here is derived from the author's lifespan, capped at fifty years after birth. That is a proxy and it is sometimes badly wrong: E. M. Forster wrote A Room with a View in 1908 and died in 1970, so a naive death-date would call a 117-year-old book 56 years old. The cap reduces that error without removing it. Ancient authors carry conventional dates - Homer is filed at 700 BC, which is a scholarly convention rather than a fact.

The download figure is thirty days, and it has a floor

Gutenberg documents the number as "the approximate number of downloads in the last 30 days (updated daily)". That is a genuine strength - it measures current readership rather than accumulating since upload, so there is no advantage to having been online longer. The weaknesses are that a thirty-day window is noisy and seasonal, set texts spike in term time, and %%BASELINE%%% of the corpus sits in a narrow band between 300 and 600 downloads a month which is too uniform to be readers. What that baseline is, Gutenberg does not say. The slider exists because of it.

Nothing after 1929, and a thin middle

Gutenberg is public domain, so the recent decades - where most forgetting actually happens - are structurally missing. There is also a genuine hollow through the medieval centuries: %%MEDIEVAL%% works between 500 and 1300 AD, against %%C19%% from the 1800s alone. A gap in the chart there is the catalogue, not a rendering fault.

It is mostly English, and one work can appear many times

%%ENSHARE%%% of the corpus is English, so this is really a claim about what English readers still open. Gutenberg also holds multiple editions and translations of the same text as separate records - four Homers appear in the oldest dozen - so a canonical work can have its readership split across several entries, which pushes the famous ones down rather than up.

How it is built

One request to Gutenberg's RDF feed gives the whole catalogue with download counts and author dates; the gutendex API serves the same numbers but only 32 books per page, which is 2,472 requests. Everything is computed once at build time into a JSON file and the page is static. Correlations are Spearman with tie correction, which matters here because the baseline band creates tens of thousands of ties. Rebuilt weekly, and each run appends a snapshot so the seasonality becomes visible over time.
