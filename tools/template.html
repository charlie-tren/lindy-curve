The Lindy Curve

Old things are supposed to endure: what has lasted a century is a fair bet to last another. Books make that testable, because Project Gutenberg publishes how often each of its texts was read this month. Here are %%WORKS%% of them, by age.

The same works as a shelf

One bar per step of age, tallest first-known work in each, oldest on the right. Colour is the century. If age drove readership the skyline would slope; it does not, and the gap in the middle is a real hollow in Gutenberg's medieval holdings rather than a missing bar.

Age vs downloads, Spearman +0.110

What the measurement actually says

Nothing much, and that is the finding. Across the whole corpus the correlation is +0.110 - older works read slightly more. Exclude the baseline band and it falls to -0.101 at a thousand downloads a month, then wanders back across zero as the threshold climbs. A relationship whose sign depends on where you draw an arbitrary line is not a relationship. On this evidence, how old a surviving book is tells you almost nothing about how much it is read.

Which is not quite the same as saying the Lindy effect is wrong. It says this dataset cannot test it - and the reason is in the methodology below, because the books that failed the test are not in the data at all.

What this cannot show

The books that died are missing

The Lindy claim is about survival, and a catalogue of survivors holds no failures. All %%ANCIENT%% works older than five hundred years are here because someone chose to digitise them. That is a selection decision, not the passage of time. It is reason enough on its own not to read a verdict off these charts.

Dates are the author's, not the work's

Gutenberg publishes no publication date, so dates come from the author's lifespan capped at birth plus fifty. Forster wrote A Room with a View in 1908 and died in 1970; the cap turns a 62-year error into 21. Homer sits at 700 BC by convention.

The count is thirty days, with a floor

Gutenberg's figure is "the approximate number of downloads in the last 30 days". Current readership, which is the strength - but seasonal, and %%BASELINE%%% of the corpus sits between 300 and 600 a month, too uniform to be readers. Cause undocumented. Hence the slider.

Nothing after 1929, thin in the middle

Public domain only, so the decades where most forgetting happens are absent. And %%MEDIEVAL%% works span 500 to 1300 AD against %%C19%% from the 1800s alone - the gap is the catalogue, not a bug.

Mostly English, and editions repeat

%%ENSHARE%%% English. Translations and editions are separate records, so a canonical work's readers split across several entries - which pushes the famous ones down.

How it is built

One request to Gutenberg's RDF feed for the whole catalogue; gutendex serves the same numbers 32 at a time. Computed once at build time, static page, Spearman with tie correction because the baseline creates tens of thousands of ties. Rebuilt weekly.
